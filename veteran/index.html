Before you dial · pickup rules, pre-call text, voicemail

Getting them to pick up

  • Use a local, registered number. Call from a number with their area code, and register your numbers with the carriers' caller ID programs so they don't show up as "Spam Likely." A vet won't answer an unknown out-of-state number.
  • Text first. Send the pre-call text below a few minutes before you dial, so your name is on their phone before it rings.
  • Call twice. If they don't answer, leave the voicemail, then call back in a few minutes like the voicemail says. A second call tells them a real person is trying to reach them.
  • Use Mr. or Ms. Older vets expect respect. Start with Mr. or Ms. [Last Name] and only switch to their first name if they tell you to.
Pre-call text

Send this a few minutes before every call. It's what makes "Did you get my text?" true.

Important

Only open with "Did you get my text?" if you actually sent one. If they catch you on a text you never sent, the call is over.

Voicemail

Leave this if they don't pick up, then call back in a few minutes.

"Hey, this is [Agent]. Not a robot, I promise. I'm following up with you on the life insurance request you filled out a while back. I'll give you a quick call in a few minutes. My state license number is [number] if you need to look me up. Talk to you soon."

How to talk to veterans · why they're on edge, and what works · agent notes

Why the hostility is there before you say a word. Three things stack up on an aged veteran lead. First, they've been trained to treat your call as a threat: AARP found 35% of military and veteran adults have lost money to a scam versus 25% of civilians, the worst offenders are benefits and "update your military record" pitches, and the VA tells them flat out that it never calls. Second, a real minority are clinically on edge: about one in six Vietnam-era vets has had PTSD, and 5 to 11% still meet criteria 40 years later; hypervigilance and a short fuse with strangers are part of that. Third, military culture itself: bottom line up front, respect for competence, contempt for fluff. The gruffness is a filter, not a verdict. It's testing whether you fold, flatter, or push. The research below is consistent on what passes the test.

  • Give them control, out loud. Trauma-informed guidance for working with veterans says to offer decisions early and often and to explain what you need, why, and what happens to it. Motivational interviewing, which the VA uses everywhere, calls it autonomy: "it's totally up to you." That's why the opener now says "you can cut me off any time" and why the review opens with what you're going to ask and why.
  • Reason before question, and never a yes-or-no exit. Say why you're calling in one breath, then ask the one question whose "yes" means go. Hostage negotiators flip it the other way on purpose: people feel safe saying no, so if "Fair enough?" is getting hang-ups, try "Is it a bad time to take five minutes to make sure it was set up right?" A vet's "no" there is a yes, and it costs him nothing to say.
  • Label it, don't fight it. "Sounds like you've been getting hammered with these calls." Naming what he's feeling lowers it; arguing raises it. Motivational interviewing calls arguing the righting reflex and says it's the single fastest way to harden resistance. Agree, one reason, one question.
  • Low, slow, downward. The calm late-night radio voice is the negotiator's default for a reason: a rising, fast sales cadence reads as pitch and triggers the hang-up reflex. Match his pace, not his volume.
  • Mirror instead of defending. Repeat his last few words back as a question ("none of my business?") and wait. He'll explain, and the explanation is the objection you can answer.
  • "What" and "how," never "why." "Why" puts people on trial. "What did they go over with you?" gets an answer.
  • Don't thank him for his service unless he brings it up. Half of veterans say it makes them uncomfortable (49%, Cohen Veterans Network), and from a stranger selling something it reads as a tactic. Veteran-services guidance also says to avoid framing their service as sacrifice or patriotic duty; many have mixed feelings about it. Never ask about combat, PTSD, or the war.
  • Show competence, not warmth. He trusts people who know their business. Specifics do the work: his VGLI band, what rescinded means, the VALife numbers, your license number in the text. Don't pretend to know military terms you don't; ask.
  • Short, concrete, formal. Mr. or Ms. [Last Name], sir or ma'am, short sentences, no elderspeak, no small talk before the point.
Where this comes from

Scam targeting: AARP / USPIS survey (35% vs 25% lost money; benefits and record-update scams). PTSD persistence: National Vietnam Veterans Longitudinal Study (15 to 17% lifetime; 4.5 to 11.2% current four decades on). "Thank you for your service": Cohen Veterans Network 2019. Trust, control and what to avoid: Swords to Plowshares, trauma-informed guidance for working with veterans; War on the Rocks, How to Talk to a Veteran. Autonomy, asking permission and the righting reflex: motivational interviewing, used across VA programs (MI overview). Labeling, mirroring, the "no" dynamic and the calm voice: Chris Voss, FBI hostage negotiation.

Who the veteran is · why they'll pick you · agent notes
Who the veteran is · why they'll pick you

Why this is the number one call lead. A veteran doesn't need to be convinced he has a gap; he already knows. SGLI (up to $500,000 for about $26 a month) ends 120 days after he gets out. If he converted to VGLI, it's five-year term that reprices every time he ages into a new band: $100,000 of VGLI is $29 a month at 50, $85 at 60, $138 at 65, $215 at 70, $385 at 75. The ads he clicked speak to exactly that: "coverage after SGLI," "is VGLI still worth it," "no exam." Vendors say these leads answer and respond better than general life leads, and the agents who hit on them say the same thing: vets keep appointments. They're used to structure.

Who's on the other end. About 17 million living veterans; roughly half are 65 and up. One in three (about 6 million) draws VA disability compensation: tax-free, deposited on the 1st, counts as income for the carriers. The most common rated conditions are tinnitus, knees, back, hearing loss, PTSD and migraines. Most of that underwrites fine. PTSD is case by case, not a decline. A lot of their care is at the VA, and VA records usually don't show up in the prescription databases carriers check. That's why policies written sloppy get approved and then rescinded; it's the whole Track A talk track.

Why they're looking. One of four things, almost every time: the VGLI bill jumped again; SGLI ended and nothing replaced it; somebody passed and they saw what a funeral costs; or they want to make sure the spouse isn't the one writing the check. A lot of them also believe "the VA takes care of my burial." It takes care of the plot and the headstone at a national cemetery. The funeral home bill is on the family, and the VA burial allowance is $978 for a non-service-connected death, $2,000 if service-connected, paid back to the family afterward.

Why they hang up. The military community reported $685 million in fraud losses in 2025, and the top warning they're given is "the VA will never call you." Anything that sounds like "the benefits office," "a new government program for veterans," or "your VA benefits" is the thing they've been told to hang up on. So is a scripted "thank you for your service" from a stranger. And never assume trauma: don't ask about combat, don't ask about PTSD, don't bring up the war.

Where these numbers come from

SGLI cost and the 120 days, VGLI age-band premiums: VA's VGLI rate table as republished at fedratecalc.com, MilPayTools, MOAA. VALife and S-DVI: Congressional Research Service, MyMilitaryBenefits. Burial allowance: Military.com. VA pay dates: fedratecalc.com. Veteran count, disability recipients and top rated conditions: VA Claims Insider (VA data). Fraud losses and the "VA will never call you" warning: AARP / FTC. PTSD underwriting: Military Times. How vets want to be talked to: War on the Rocks. Lead-side tips: Agent Lead Lab, Stallion Leads, Life Jacket Leads; those are vendors, so treat them as the shape, not the decimal. Funeral cost: NFDA 2023 median, $8,300 with viewing and burial before the cemetery.

The opener, written out

"Hello, Mr./Ms. [Last Name]? [pause] Hey, how's it going? This is [Agent]. Did you ever get my text by chance?"

Pause

Let them talk. Most say "About what?" or "What text?" That's not a no; it's the normal answer.

If they say "About what?" / no:

"Yeah, it was about the life insurance request you sent in for veterans a while back. You've probably got something in place by now, and that's fine, I'm not selling you anything. The reason I'm calling is we've been seeing a lot of veteran policies get rescinded, cancelled after the fact, and my job is just to make sure yours was set up right, because at the end of the day, if the claim gets denied, it ain't worth a dang thing. Right? [pause] So if it was set up right, I close your file and you won't hear from me again. Five minutes, and you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Important

No question until the reason is out, and the only question is "Fair enough?", where yes means go. Don't ask who it's with yet; vets guard that from a stranger, and "none of your business" is the answer you'll get. The carrier comes up in the review, after the branch question. Say the whole thing in one breath; the hang-up happens in the gap.

If they say yes:

"Good. I'll be quick. You've probably got something in place by now, and that's fine, I'm not selling you anything. The reason I'm calling is we've been seeing a lot of veteran policies get rescinded, cancelled after the fact, and my job is just to make sure yours was set up right, because at the end of the day, if the claim gets denied, it ain't worth a dang thing. Right? [pause] So if it was set up right, I close your file and you won't hear from me again. Five minutes, and you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

If they say "I'm all set":

"I'm sure you are, and that's exactly what I'm checking. If it was set up right, I close the file and you never hear from me again. Five minutes. Fair enough?"

Note

Agree fast, one reason, "Fair enough?", no gap. Don't ask for the carrier here. A second "I'm all set" is the second no.

If they say "None of your business" / "I'll talk to my agent":

"Understood, and I'm not asking who it's with. The only thing I check is whether the health questions were read to you one at a time, because that's what's getting veteran policies cancelled. If they were, you're good and I'm gone. Fair enough?"

Note

Don't argue and don't repeat the question they just refused. "Stop calling me" is the list, today. A hang-up gets one redial later today with the voicemail; it's a reflex, not a decision.

If your list is running hot · the name-it-first version:

"You're probably thinking this is another one of those calls. I get it, you've been getting hammered. It's not a pitch. Important

Track A · Policy review (most calls)

1. The hook

"Okay, great. So real quick, what rescinded means: the insurance company cancels the policy like it never existed. They send back what you paid, and the family gets nothing when they need it, right? And it usually happens because the health questions weren't answered correctly on the application. So that's all I'm checking for."

Note

Short, because the reason for the call already went out in the opener. The "right?" tag is from Nick's live call, 10/31. Then straight into the review questions; the first one is the branch question, and the carrier comes after that. When they name the carrier: "When's the last time your agent called you just to check on it?" Most will say never. Let that land. If everything checks out, actually close the file and mark them do-not-call.

If they say "Not right now" / "I'm in the middle of something" (at the opener or here):

"Okay, got it. Is there a better time later today, or tomorrow?"

Pause

Let them name it. If they say "whenever," you pick two: "Later today around [time], or tomorrow morning at [time]?" A day with no time is not an appointment.

"[Day] at [time]? [pause] Alright, great. Can you do me a favor real quick? I'm going to send you a confirmation text. My name's [Agent]. Just reply with the letter C for confirm, okay? All my credentials will be on there. Alright, sir, have a good one."

Confirmation text · send it before your next dial
Important

Same day or tomorrow, at a specific time, and the text goes out before you dial the next lead. Call back on the dot. If they don't reply C, call anyway; the text did its job by putting your name and license on their phone.

2. Review questions

"I'm going to ask about the policy, not your personal business: who it's with, how much, and whether the health questions were read to you. That's it."

Note

Say what you're going to ask and why before you ask it. That one sentence is what keeps "none of your business" from showing up in the review. The moment they say they don't have a policy at all, stop and switch to Track B.

Ask these one at a time and write down the answers:

  • "First, what branch did you serve in?" (Let them talk. Thank them once.)
  • "Do you have the policy paperwork handy? Grab it if you can, it makes this quick."
  • "What's the name of the insurance company it's with?"
  • "How much coverage is it for?"
  • "And how much are you paying for it a month?"
  • "Do you know if it's permanent, or does it run out at a certain age?"
  • "Has the price ever gone up on you?"
  • "Did they tell you about a waiting period, where it only pays in full after the first two or three years?"
  • "Did you apply over the phone, or did someone come out to see you?"
  • "Who's your beneficiary, and is that still who you'd want?"
  • "Does your family know the policy exists and where to find it?"

3. What to listen for

Their coverage amount and monthly premium

Compare what they pay per $1,000 of coverage with what you can get them today. Paying a lot for a little coverage is your opening to show a better option.

"There's a waiting period"

They may be on a guaranteed or graded policy. If their health now qualifies, a policy that pays in full from day one may be available.

"It goes up" or "it's VGLI"

VGLI and many term policies get more expensive with age. Permanent coverage with a locked price may fit better.

"It ends at 80" or "it's through work"

Term coverage can run out, and work coverage usually ends with the job.

"My ex is the beneficiary"

An easy, real fix, even if you sell nothing.

"They didn't really go over health questions"

Use the health-answer talk track below.

4. Health-answer talk track

"One thing I check on every review. When you applied, did the agent read you each health question, or kind of fly through it?"

"Here's why I ask. Your VA health history is protected, so most carriers can't see it when you apply. If an agent just marks no on every health question, the policy can still get approved. The problem comes later. The carrier can pull those VA records, and if they don't match the application, they can cancel the policy like it never existed, and your family gets nothing. We've seen it happen to veterans more than once. It takes two minutes to make sure yours was done right."

Important

Rules for this talk track: Never say or suggest their agent lied. Never put a number on how often this happens. If their answers were wrong, the honest fix is a correctly answered new application. Never let them cancel the old policy until the new one is approved.

5. Close the review

"Here's what I found: [one or two specific things]. I can see if there's an option that fixes that. If there isn't, you keep what you have and you'll know it's solid. Want me to check?"

If they say "No, I'm fine":

"Fair enough. One thing before I close it out. [The specific thing], that's the one that costs your family, not you. Two minutes to see if it's fixable, and if the number isn't better, you keep exactly what you have. Fair?"

Note

Name the one thing you found; don't repeat the whole review. A second no here goes to Second no in Pushback. If the review was clean, say so, close the file, and mark them do-not-call. That's not a loss; it's the promise you made in the hook.

Go

Then go to Explain How It Works (Both Tracks). If you replace a policy, complete your state's replacement paperwork and only replace it if the new policy is truly better for them.

Coverage Options and Quote.

1. Coverage types

"Alright, now we're done with the health and financial questions. Did you want me to go over the different types of coverage, or did you want me to just do what I think is best?"

Pause

Wait for their answer. If they say "do what's best," skip the explanation and go to the recommendation line below.

"Let me go ahead and explain the main types:"

  • "Whole life is permanent coverage that lasts your whole life. The coverage amount and the payment stay the same and never change. This is the type people use for final expenses."
Important

(If health is bad, do not go over IUL or term. Skip the next two.)

  • "IUL, or indexed universal life, is permanent coverage that builds cash value you can use down the road."
  • "Term life covers you for a set number of years. The coverage and payment stay level during that time. This type gives your family the most coverage for the money."
  • "Accidental death only pays if you pass away from an accident, like a car accident, a fall or being hit by a car."
Note

Accidental death: offer it to clients under 59 and a half. If you hold an accident and health license, you can write Mutual of Omaha accidental death up to age 70.

"Based on your health, your finances and your situation, I'd recommend [whole life / term life]. I'm going to do some shopping around and see what we can do for you."

"While I do that, do you have a pen and paper handy? I'm going to have you write down some important things."

Pause

Wait until they have it ready.

2. Present the carrier

"Okay, so I found the carrier coming in with the highest chance of approval at the lowest monthly price out of all the carriers I work with. The company that's willing to entertain your application today is [CARRIER]. I use the word entertain because I can't guarantee an approval just yet, but they're showing up as a promising option for us."

"Alright, I'm going to show you the three options [CARRIER] is recommending. These are based on what most people in your same age and health range choose, but we can move the coverage amount around. My goal today is to find something that fits your needs, but most importantly your budget. Okay?"

3. Whole life only

"All of these options are permanent policies, meaning they never expire. You'll have this policy for the rest of your life, and it's locked in. Your payment will never go up and your coverage will never go down. Alright?"

Important

Use this only for whole life. Don't read it for IUL. IUL payments are flexible, the costs inside the policy can rise, and it can lapse if it's underfunded unless it has a no-lapse guarantee. For IUL, explain the specific product's guarantees instead.

Important

If it's a graded or guaranteed issue policy, tell them about the waiting period before the full benefit kicks in. Hiding it is exactly the problem Track A finds on other agents' policies.

R · Responses to common pushback

Responses to Common Pushback.

Every response ends in a question where yes means go, almost always "Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?" Never a question whose yes is an exit ("you've got something in place, right?"), and never "who's it with?" before they've agreed to the review. Two more rules run the section: a file closes on the third no, not the first, and never offer to take someone off the list; if they ask, do it. If they can't talk, book a specific time today or tomorrow and confirm it by text.

"I'm all set." (then they hang up)

"I'm sure you are, and that's exactly what I'm checking. If it was set up right, I close the file and you never hear from me again. Five minutes. Fair enough?"

Important

This is the number one hang-up on aged veteran leads, and it's caused by the question before it. "You've probably got something in place, right?" is a yes-or-no question asked before they have a reason to stay, so "yeah, I'm all set" is the natural exit. The fix is in the opener: the reason for the call comes first, in the same breath, and the only question is "Fair enough?", where yes means go. Don't ask who it's with until the review; a stranger asking for the carrier gets "none of your business." Second "I'm all set" goes to Second no. A hang-up gets one redial later today, with the voicemail.

"None of your business." / "I'll talk to my agent directly." / "It's not your problem."

"Understood, and I'm not asking who it's with. The only thing I check is whether the health questions were read to you one at a time, because that's what's getting veteran policies cancelled. If they were, you're good and I'm gone. Fair enough?"

Note

Don't argue and don't repeat the question they just refused. Give them the one thing you check, then "Fair enough?" "I'll talk to my agent": "Good, and ask him that exact question. If he read them to you one by one, you're solid. If he flew through it, that's the policy that gets cancelled." Then "Fair enough?" again. "Stop calling me" is the list, today.

"Who the hell is this?" / "Stop calling me!" (angry)

"Sounds like you've been getting hammered with these calls. [pause, let him vent] I'm not with the VA and I'm not selling anything. One thing, then I'm gone: when they set up your life insurance, did they read you the health questions one at a time? That's what's getting veteran policies cancelled."

Important

Label it first, then let him vent; naming what he's feeling lowers it, arguing raises it. Low, slow voice, no "why" questions, don't match his volume. One reason, one question, and his control is explicit ("then I'm gone"). A second "stop calling me" is the list, today, no exceptions.

"Are you with the VA?"

"No sir, thank you for asking. I'm a licensed independent agent, what they call the private sector. Most vets I talk to either had SGLI while they were in, or switched to VGLI when they got out. VGLI is term coverage, and the price goes up every five years as you get older, so it can get expensive. My job is to make sure you're not overpaying. I work with [number] carriers, so I can shop it around and show you if there's something better. And if what you have is the best deal, I'll tell you that too. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Note

Fill in your real carrier count. Don't say "all the carriers in the nation." SGLI only covers people while they serve, so don't describe SGLI as increasing. That's VGLI. End on "Fair enough?", never on "you've got something in place, right?"; that yes is a hang-up.

"Not interested."

"Are we talking about the same thing? [pause] This is the life insurance request you put in for veterans, about [a year] ago. Did someone already go over it with you, did you not qualify, or was it too expensive?"

Note

Soft, a little surprised. Don't accept the first no. Most of the time they think this is a VA call or a cold call about something else. Whatever they answer is a branch: already set up → "I already have coverage." Didn't qualify → "I didn't qualify." Too expensive → "Fixed income." Don't need it → "I don't need it." A flat second "not interested" → Second no.

"I get these calls nonstop."

"I believe it. Veterans get called more than anyone in this business, and most of those calls are the same pitch. This isn't a pitch. It's a review of what you already have, it's five minutes, and when it's done your file's closed on my end. You can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Important

Don't knock the other callers. Don't offer to take them off the list; if they ask, do it today. Don't ask who it's with here. The line that lands at the end of the call: "Stop filling out the veteran forms online. Every one gives another agent consent to call you."

"I already have coverage." / "It's fine, I'm good."

"Good, that's exactly why I'm calling. I'm not here to sell you a second policy. I'm here to make sure the one you have actually pays out the way you think. When's the last time someone went over it with you?"

If they say "It's fine, I'm good":

"I believe you, and most of the time it is. The one thing I check is whether the health questions were read to you one by one, because that's what gets policies cancelled after the fact, and on veterans it happens more than anywhere else. Five minutes, and if it's clean, you know it's solid. Fair enough?"

Note

Don't argue about the policy, and don't ask who it's with yet; that comes in the review. A second "I'm good" is the second no.

"My agent takes care of me."

"Glad to hear it. Then this is a second set of eyes and it costs you nothing. If everything checks out, you'll know your agent did right by you. When's the last time he called you just to check on it?"

Note

Most say never. Let that land, then the hook. "He checks in all the time" → treat it as "It's fine, I'm good."

"I have VGLI." / "I kept my military insurance."

"Okay, good, that's the group policy you kept from the service. How much is it for, and what's it running you a month right now? [pause] And has the price gone up on you since you got out?"

Note

VGLI is five-year term, so the price steps up every five years. Per $100,000 a month: $29 at 50, $50 at 55, $85 at 60, $138 at 65, $215 at 70, $385 at 75, $440 at 80. Know their next band; don't lecture them with it. Let them tell you it's gone up, then the hook, same as any policy. VGLI never gets cancelled until the new policy is approved; for a lot of vets the right answer is keep it and add a small permanent policy underneath it.

"I don't remember filling anything out." / "I never did that."

"That's fair, it was a while back. It was a form about life insurance for veterans, and I've got you in [State], born in [Year], so it's you. Here's why I'm calling: a lot of veteran policies have been getting rescinded, cancelled after the fact, and my job is just to make sure yours was set up right. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

If they insist they never did:

"Could be a family member filled it out for you, it happens a lot. Either way it's on my desk with your name on it, so let me at least make sure your file's right. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Note

Don't argue about the form. Wrong person: "Sorry about that, I'll fix it on my end. Have a good day." Mark the file.

"How did you get my number?" / "What's this about?"

"From the request you sent in for life insurance for veterans, about [a year] ago. I've got you in [State], born in [Year]. That's why I texted you first instead of just dialing. And the reason I'm calling is we've been seeing a lot of veteran policies get rescinded, cancelled after the fact; my job is just to make sure yours was set up right. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

"Sure. A while back you sent in a request for life insurance for veterans. Most of the vets I talk to already have something, so my job is to make sure it was set up right, and if it was, I close your file and you don't hear from me again. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Two versions of the same answer. Either way, end on "Fair enough?"

"Is this a scam?"

"Fair question, and I'd rather you ask it than not. My name and license number are in the text I sent you. Pull it up and look me up on the state insurance department website while we talk. I'm not the VA, and I'm not calling about your VA benefits. This is the life insurance request you sent in for veterans, and all I'm doing is making sure what you have was set up right. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Note

Calm and straight. If they never got the text, resend it now, while they're on the line.

"Just mail me something."

"I hear you. Here's the thing, though. There are a million different quotes out there, and they're all just starting points. Until the application actually goes in, it's like throwing spaghetti at the wall and hoping it sticks. I could mail you ten quotes today, but if you don't get approved for them, we're back to the drawing board. The only way to know your real price is to submit it and see what the carrier comes back with. Let me ask you a few quick questions so we're working with real numbers, not guesses. Fair enough?"

If they still want it mailed:

"Totally fine. Two quick things so what I send is real and not a guess: who's the coverage with now, and about how much is it for? [pause] Okay. And has the price ever gone up on you?"

Note

You're in the review questions now. Keep going; the mail never gets mentioned again.

"I'm busy." / "I'm at work." / "I'm in the middle of something."

"Gotcha, not a problem. I only need your ear, five to ten minutes, and you can cut me off any time. Real quick, here's why I'm calling." (then the opener's reason line and "Fair enough?")

If they really can't:

"Okay, got it. Is there a better time later today, or tomorrow? [pause] [Day] at [time]? Alright, great. Can you do me a favor real quick? I'm going to send you a confirmation text. My name's [Agent]. Just reply with the letter C for confirm, okay? All my credentials will be on there."

Important

Same day or tomorrow, at a specific time; never "later this week." Send the confirmation text before your next dial and call back on the dot. This is how Nick handled it on the 10/31 call: no push, book it, confirm by text.

"I have a $10,000 VA policy I don't pay for."
Note

This is usually the VA's S-DVI policy. Totally disabled vets can have the premiums waived on up to $10,000. The VA stopped taking new S-DVI applications at the end of 2022, so it can't be replaced. Never tell them to cancel it.

"That's great, and honestly, keep that policy. It's free, and you can't get it anymore, so whatever we do, that one stays exactly where it is."

"Can I ask you something? Who's the beneficiary on it, and have you told them it exists?"

"Here's why I ask. A funeral with a burial runs about $8,300 these days, and that's before the cemetery, the headstone and everything else. So that $10,000 pretty much covers the funeral and that's it. If you passed tomorrow, would you want to leave anything beyond the funeral? Help with bills, the house, something for your [wife/kids]?"

If they say no:

"Fair enough. Let me ask it one other way. The day that $10,000 goes to the funeral home, what does [Beneficiary] have for the next couple of months? [pause] That's the piece we'd look at, and it's small. Worth two minutes to see the number?"

Note

One more ask, then respect it. Second no: "Then you're in good shape, sir. Just make sure your beneficiary knows where the paperwork is." Close the file.

If they say yes:

"Then let's see what we can add on top of what you have. Nothing replaces it."

Note

A 100% disabled vet will often only qualify for graded or guaranteed issue whole life, so disclose the waiting period. If VALife fits them better, tell them: it's the VA's guaranteed acceptance whole life, up to $40,000 for vets with a disability rating, with a two-year waiting period and premiums they pay.

"I'll just go through the VA." / "I'll get it from the VA directly."

"I understand, and if the VA had a good fit for you I'd tell you to take it. Here's what most vets don't know. For somebody who's already out, the VA only has two life insurance programs. VGLI, you had to grab within about a year of getting out, and it's five-year term that goes up every five years. The other one is VALife. It's only for vets with a disability rating, it tops out at $40,000, it's priced like you're the sickest guy in the room, and it doesn't pay the full amount until you've had it two years. So a healthy 60-year-old pays $200 a month for $40,000 and his family waits two years for it. That's the gap the form you filled out was about. Quick question: do you have a disability rating with the VA?"

No rating:

"Then the VA doesn't have a policy for you. That's not me saying it, that's their eligibility. So let's see what you do qualify for."

Go

Into Qualifying. If they have coverage elsewhere, run Track A first.

Yes, I have a rating:

"Then VALife is one of your options, and I'll put it next to what I've got, side by side. If it wins, take it. Let me ask a few health questions first, because if you're in decent shape you'll likely get day-one coverage for less."

Note

If their health is bad, VALife may beat a graded private policy. Say so; that's what makes the review real.

Important

Never knock the VA. Agree first, then explain the two doors. If they say "SGLI," they mean VGLI; SGLI ended when they got out. VALife for $40,000 a month: $130 at 50, $200 at 60, $312 at 70, $510 at 80, same price healthy or not, two-year wait, and it needs a service-connected rating (any percent, 0 included) at 80 or under. VGLI had to be taken within 1 year and 120 days of separation. S-DVI closed to new applicants at the end of 2022.

"The VA takes care of my burial."

"You're right about part of it. If you go in a national cemetery, the plot, the headstone and the flag are covered. What's not covered is the funeral home: the casket or the cremation, the service, the transportation. That bill is [Beneficiary]'s, and the VA burial allowance for it is about $978, $2,000 if it's service-connected, and the family pays everything up front and files for it afterward. So on day three, who's writing the funeral home the check? [pause] Do you have anything in place for that right now?"

Note

The plot, not the funeral home. Say it once, don't lecture. Then the fork. National cemetery burial, headstone and flag are free for eligible vets; the funeral home bill is the family's. Burial allowance: about $978 for a non-service-connected death (deaths on or after Oct 1, 2024) plus a $978 plot allowance if they're not in a national cemetery; $2,000 if service-connected; paid as a reimbursement.

"My wife gets my benefits." / "I'm 100%, she's taken care of."

"Okay, and that matters, so let me make sure I understand what she'd actually get. Your disability pay stops the month you pass. If your passing is service-connected, or you've been rated 100% for ten years, she can apply for DIC, about $1,700 a month, tax-free, and it takes weeks to months to start. If it's not, there's no monthly check. Either way, nobody's paying the funeral home in the first week. So the question is still the same one: on day three, who's writing that check?"

Important

Don't say "you won't get it." Say "it takes time and it doesn't pay the funeral home." DIC base rate for 2026 is about $1,700 a month; eligibility is service-connected death, or total disability for 10 years before death (5 years if from discharge, 1 year if a former POW). Don't quote rules you're not sure of; the point is the gap in week one.

"I've got money saved." / "My kids will handle it."

"Good, you're ahead of most people. Quick question though: is that money sitting where [Beneficiary] could get to it the week after, or is it in a TSP, a 401k, the house, something like that? [pause] A funeral is $8,300 before the cemetery, and none of that pays a funeral home on day three. You can hand that out of the savings, or a small policy pays in days, tax-free, straight to [Beneficiary], and the savings never get touched. Worth a couple of questions to see the number?"

Note

Everyone says they have savings. Make them say where it is. If it's real money sitting in a bank doing nothing, that's the annuity conversation after the policy, not before.

"I'm on a fixed income." / "I can't afford anything." / "It was too expensive."

"Then this matters more, not less. If what you have is solid, I'll tell you and you keep it. If you're overpaying, that's money back in your pocket every month. And if you have nothing, we start with the smallest option and it has to fit the budget or I won't put you in it. Five minutes, you can cut me off any time. Fair enough?"

Note

Budget is the first thing you ask in Qualifying anyway. Never promise a price here.

"I'm too old." / "They won't cover me."

"They will. Age changes the price, not the yes. I've got carriers that go up to 89, and I'm not asking for a medical exam. Let me ask you a couple quick questions and I'll tell you straight what's there. Fair?"

Note

Over 80: fewer carriers, smaller amounts. Say it before the numbers, not after.

"I didn't qualify." / "I got declined."

"Really? Did they tell you why? [pause] Every carrier reads the same condition differently, and I work with [number] of them. Let me ask a couple quick health questions and I'll tell you straight if I've got one for you. Fair?"

Note

Into Track B; the health questions in Qualifying decide it. If nothing's day one, a graded plan and VALife (with a VA rating) are both honest answers.

"I don't need it."

"Okay, help me understand that, because you filled this out, so something had you looking. What's different now?"

Note

They're trying to get you off the phone. Make them say the actual reason, then answer that reason: the VA burial, the savings, the kids, a policy they got since. "Just don't want it" → Second no.

Second no / third no · the close-out rule
Second no:

"Fair enough. Last thing, and I'm only asking because it changes what I do with your file: was it the price, or you just don't need it anymore?"

Third no:

"Understood. I'll make a note on the file. If anything changes, you've got my number."

Important

The second no gets one more question, and the answer is always a branch (price → Fixed income; don't need it → I don't need it). The third no closes the file. You never offer to close it yourself, and you never say "I'll take you off the list" unless they ask. If they ask, do it today.
